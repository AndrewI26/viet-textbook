# Tones

In Vietnamese, the *pitch* of your voice is part of the word. Say [[ma]] with your voice flat and it means "ghost". Let your voice rise and it becomes [[má]], "mom". This chapter teaches the five tones of the South, one at a time, and lets you check your own.
{: .lead }

## 3.1 What is a tone?

A tone is the **movement of your voice's pitch** across a syllable: staying level, rising, falling, or dipping and coming back up.

English uses pitch too, but for feelings rather than meaning. "Coffee?" rises because it's a question, and "Coffee." falls because it's a statement. Either way it still means coffee. In Vietnamese, changing the pitch changes the word.

Two things make tones easier than they first sound:

- **Tones are relative to *your* voice.** There's no "correct" note to hit. A man and a child say the same tone at very different pitches; what matters is the shape.
- **Every syllable has exactly one tone,** marked on its main vowel. Once you know the marks, you always know which tone to use.

## 3.2 The five Southern tones

Vietnamese writing has six tones: five marks, plus no mark. In the South, **hỏi and ngã are said the same way**, so you only need to *hear and say* five.

## 3.3 Each tone, step by step

For each tone, read the tip, listen, then press **Record**. The chart draws your pitch as you speak and compares it with the shape. The first time, it asks for a flat "aaa" to learn your normal voice level.

### Ngang: level

Hold one steady note in the middle of your voice. The trap for English speakers is letting it drop at the end, the way an English sentence does. Keep it up until the sound stops.

Listen: [[ba]] (three) · [[tên]] (name) · [[anh]] (older brother) · [[chơi]] (to play)

<say-it>[[ba]]</say-it>

### Sắc: rising

Start in the middle and rise sharply, like a surprised "What?!" Let the air flow freely while your voice climbs.

Listen: [[má]] (mom) · [[có]] (to have) · [[nói]] (to speak) · [[sáng]] (morning)

<say-it>[[có]]</say-it>

### Huyền: falling

Start a little below the middle and let your voice fall gently and stay low. It's the easiest tone for English speakers: it's how a calm English sentence ends.

Listen: [[mà]] (but) · [[bà]] (grandmother) · [[chào]] (hello) · [[người]] (person)

<say-it>[[bà]]</say-it>

### Hỏi and ngã: dipping

Go down first, then come back up above where you started. *Hỏi* means "to ask", and this tone sounds a bit like a doubtful "Huh?"

Listen to hỏi: [[mả]] (grave) · [[hỏi]] (to ask) · [[của]] (of) · [[phải]] (must; right)

Listen to ngã: [[mã]] (horse) · [[Mỹ]] (America) · [[sữa]] (milk) · [[chữ]] (letter, written word)

<say-it>[[hỏi]]</say-it>

<say-it>[[sữa]]</say-it>

<div class="south" markdown="1">
In the North (Hà Nội), these are two different tones: **hỏi** falls low, and **ngã** rises with a little catch, almost a break, in the throat. In the South they're one dipping tone. Learn to *recognise* the Northern ones, but say the Southern one.
</div>

### Nặng: heavy

Drop low, as if the word is heavy. In the South it dips and comes back up only a little, and it never climbs as high as hỏi. When a word ends in **-p, -t, -c** or **-ch**, there's no time to come back up, so it's just a short, low drop.

Listen: [[mạ]] (rice seedling) · [[mẹ]] (mother) · [[chị]] (older sister) · [[một]] (one) · [[học]] (to study)

<say-it>[[mẹ]]</say-it>

<say-it>[[học]]</say-it>

<div class="south" markdown="1">
In the North, nặng is cut off with a catch in the throat, like a tiny cough. The Southern nặng is smoother.
</div>

<div class="tip" markdown="1">
**Short words ending in -p, -t, -c, -ch** can only carry **sắc** or **nặng**. That makes them easier: it's either a quick high rise, [[hát]] (to sing), or a quick low drop, [[hạt]] (seed).
</div>

## 3.4 Where the mark goes

A tone mark always sits on a vowel. When a syllable has more than one vowel, follow these rules, in this order:

1. **One vowel:** the mark goes on it. [[mã]], [[bạn]].
2. **A vowel with its own mark wins** (ă, â, ê, ô, ơ, ư): [[tiếng]], [[Việt]], [[tuổi]], [[người]]. In *ươ*, it's the *ơ*.
3. **Two vowels with nothing after them:** the first one. [[hai]], [[mía]], [[của]].
4. **Vowels followed by a consonant:** the last vowel. [[toán]] (math), [[khoảng]] (about).
5. **Three vowels with no vowel marks:** the middle one. [[ngoài]] (outside), [[xoáy]] (to swirl).
6. **qu and gi:** the *u* and *i* belong to the consonant, so skip them. [[quả]] (fruit), [[giờ]] (hour).

<div class="tip" markdown="1">
You'll also see a newer style that puts the mark on the second vowel in *oa, oe, uy*: **hoà** instead of **hòa**, **thuỷ** instead of **thủy**. Both are correct. This book uses the traditional style.
</div>

<details markdown="1">

**chào**: two vowels with nothing after them, so the mark goes on the first one.
</details>

<details markdown="1">

**tuổi**: the *ô* has its own vowel mark, so it wins.
</details>

<details markdown="1">

**quả**: the *u* belongs to *qu*, so *a* is the only real vowel.

</minimal-pair>

**Hỏi vs nặng: both dip, but hỏi climbs back up high.**

<minimal-pair labels="to tell, bold">

</minimal-pair>
