# How Vietnamese works

Before learning any words, it helps to see how the language is built. Vietnamese uses the same alphabet as English, plus a few marks, and every syllable follows the same simple pattern.
{: .lead }

Every word in this book drawn like [[tiếng Việt]] is clickable. Tap it to hear it.

## 1.1 About this book

Vietnamese is spoken by around 100 million people. It has three main accents: Northern (Hà Nội), Central (Huế) and Southern (Sài Gòn, also called Ho Chi Minh City). The spelling is the same everywhere. Only the pronunciation changes.

This book teaches the **Southern accent**, the way people speak in [[Sài Gòn]] and the Mekong Delta. It's also the accent of most Vietnamese communities overseas.

<div class="south" markdown="1">
A few things you'll hear in the South that differ from the North:

- **Five tones, not six.** Two of the six tone marks (*hỏi* and *ngã*) sound the same.
- **v, d and gi sound like English "y".** [[vui]] (happy) sounds like "yui" and [[dạ]] (yes) like "yah".
- **Some endings merge.** [[bạn]] (friend) ends like "bạng", and [[anh]] (older brother) sounds like "an".
</div>

Pronunciation is the hardest part of Vietnamese and the most important to get right early, so the first five chapters are about sounds. Words and sentences start in Chapter 6.

The audio in this book is made by a computer voice with a Southern accent. It's very good, but not perfect. If something sounds strange, trust a native speaker over the recording.

## 1.2 The alphabet

Vietnamese is written with the Latin alphabet. The writing system is called **chữ Quốc ngữ** ("national script"). It has 29 letters. There's no *f*, *j*, *w* or *z*, except in foreign names and borrowed words.

Click a letter to hear its name and an example word to hear the letter in use.

There are twelve vowel letters. Six of them have a mark built in (ă, â, ê, ô, ơ, ư). These are separate letters, not accents.

## 1.3 Anatomy of a syllable

Vietnamese is written one syllable at a time, with a space after each. Many words are one syllable, like [[ăn]] (eat). Others are two, like [[tiếng Việt]] (Vietnamese) or [[cảm ơn]] (thank you).

Every syllable has the same four parts:

1. **Start**: a consonant (optional)
2. **Vowel**: one vowel, or a group of vowels (always there)
3. **End**: a consonant or a short *-i*, *-o*, *-u*, *-y* (optional)
4. **Tone**: the pitch of your voice (always there, even when there's no mark)

| Syllable | Start | Vowel | End | Tone |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [[a]] | | a | | ngang (no mark) |
| [[ba]] | b | a | | ngang (no mark) |
| [[bán]] | b | a | n | sắc |
| [[tiếng]] | t | iê | ng | sắc |
| [[Việt]] | v | iê | t | nặng |
| [[người]] | ng | ươ | i | huyền |

Only a few consonants can end a syllable: **c, ch, m, n, ng, nh, p, t**. Chapter 5 is all about these endings, which are cut short in Vietnamese.

## 1.4 Two kinds of marks

Vietnamese uses two kinds of marks, and it's important not to mix them up.

**Vowel marks** make a different vowel. They're part of the letter. (The hints below are rough; Chapter 2 covers each vowel properly.)

<div class="state-row">
<div>[[a]]<span class="caption">"ah"</span></div>
<div>[[ă :: á]]<span class="caption">short "ah"</span></div>
<div>[[â :: ớ]]<span class="caption">short "uh"</span></div>
<div>[[o]]<span class="caption">"aw"</span></div>
<div>[[ô]]<span class="caption">"oh"</span></div>
<div>[[ơ]]<span class="caption">"uh"</span></div>
<div>[[u]]<span class="caption">"oo"</span></div>
<div>[[ư]]<span class="caption">"oo", lips wide</span></div>
</div>

**Tone marks** change the pitch of the whole syllable. There are five, plus "no mark":

| Mark | Name | Example | How it sounds (Southern) |
|---|---|---|---|
| a (none) | ngang | [[ma]] (ghost) | flat, in the middle of your voice |
| á | sắc | [[má]] (mom) | rises high |
| à | huyền | [[mà]] (but) | low, falling gently |
| ả | hỏi | [[mả]] (grave) | dips down, then rises |
| ã | ngã | [[mã]] (horse) | same as hỏi in the South |
| ạ | nặng | [[mạ]] (rice seedling) | low and heavy, with a small dip |

One syllable, six spellings, six meanings. Use "Play all" to hear them in a row:

<tone-drill labels="ngang, sắc, huyền, hỏi, ngã, nặng">
[[ma]] [[má]] [[mà]] [[mả]] [[mã]] [[mạ]]
</tone-drill>

A vowel can carry **both** kinds of mark. In [[Việt]], the *ê* has a vowel mark (ˆ) and a tone mark (the dot below). In [[người]], the *ơ* has a vowel mark and the *huyền* tone mark.

<div class="south" markdown="1">
In the South, [[mả]] and [[mã]] sound the same. You still need to learn both marks for spelling, but you only need to *hear* five tones. Chapter 3 covers each tone in detail.
</div>

## 1.5 How to practise

Your ear learns by comparing your guess to the real sound, so every time you meet a new word:

1. **Read it and guess** how it sounds, using its parts and its tone mark.
2. **Say it out loud.** Exaggerate the tone at first.
3. **Click to check,** then say it again.

Some more habits that help:

- **Record yourself** on your phone and compare it with the clickable audio.
- **Practise in pairs.** Words that differ by one sound or one tone train your ear fastest.
- **Do your flashcards every day.** Ten minutes daily works better than an hour once a week.
