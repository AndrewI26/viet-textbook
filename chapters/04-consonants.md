# Consonants

Most Vietnamese consonants are close to English ones. A handful are new, and a few pairs differ in a way English doesn't care about, such as a puff of air. This chapter covers the consonants that *start* a syllable. Chapter 5 covers the ones that end it.
{: .lead }

## 4.1 Sounds you already know

These are close enough to English that you can say them the English way and be understood.

| Letter | Like English… | Example |
|---|---|---|
| b | *b* in *bed* | [[bà]] grandmother |
| m | *m* in *me* | [[mẹ]] mother |
| n | *n* in *no* | [[năm]] five |
| l | *l* in *leaf* | [[lá]] leaf |
| h | *h* in *hat* | [[hai]] two |
| ph | *f* in *far* | [[phở]] noodle soup |
| x | *s* in *sun* | [[xin]] please |
| g | *g* in *go*, but softer | [[gà]] chicken |

<div class="tip" markdown="1">
Vietnamese **b** (and **đ**, below) are made with a tiny inward pull of air. You don't need to copy it: a normal English *b* sounds fine. Vietnamese **g** is softer than English, because the back of the tongue doesn't quite close. A gentle *g* is perfect.
</div>

## 4.2 Sounds that work differently

### đ and d: two different letters

**đ** is a firm *d*, as in *do*. **d** is a completely different sound: in the South it's like English *y*.

<minimal-pair labels="to go, aunt">
[[đi]] [[dì]]
</minimal-pair>

### t and th: the puff of air

Hold your hand in front of your mouth and say *top*, then *stop*. You feel a puff of air on *top* but not on *stop*. Vietnamese uses that difference to tell words apart:

- **t** has **no puff**, like the *t* in *stop*.
- **th** has **a puff**, like the *t* in *top*. It's never the English *th* in *think*.

<minimal-pair labels="I, enough">
[[tôi]] [[thôi]]
</minimal-pair>

<minimal-pair labels="to practise, autumn">
[[tu]] [[thu]]
</minimal-pair>

### c, k and kh

**c** and **k** are the same sound: a *k* with no puff, like the *k* in *skin*. **kh** is softer and breathier, like the *ch* in Scottish *loch*, or a gentle snore. Never let *kh* become a hard *k*.

<minimal-pair labels="fish, quite">
[[cá]] [[khá]]
</minimal-pair>

<minimal-pair labels="aunt, dry">
[[cô]] [[khô]]
</minimal-pair>

### nh: like the ñ in señor

Press the middle of your tongue flat against the roof of your mouth and say *n*. It's the *ny* in *canyon*, or the *ñ* in Spanish *señor*.

<minimal-pair labels="full, grape">
[[no]] [[nho]]
</minimal-pair>

### ng and ngh: the sound from "singing"

English has this sound, but only in the middle or at the end of a word (*singing*). Vietnamese also uses it at the start. Try this: say "singing" slowly, then drop the *si-*: "nging". That's the start of [[ngủ]] (to sleep).

<minimal-pair labels="house, ivory">
[[nhà]] [[ngà]]
</minimal-pair>

More to try: [[người]] (person), [[nghe]] (to listen), [[ngon]] (delicious)

### tr and ch

These are two different sounds in the South.

- **ch** is softer than English *ch*, with no puff, closer to the *ty* in *got you*.
- **tr** is made with the tip of your tongue curled back, so it sounds harder and fuller, like a strong English *ch*.

<minimal-pair labels="bamboo, to cover">
[[tre]] [[che]]
</minimal-pair>

More to try: [[trà]] (tea), [[trong]] (in), [[chị]] (older sister), [[cho]] (to give)

## 4.3 Southern consonants

Spelling is the same everywhere, but a few letters sound different in the South. This is where the Southern accent is easiest to hear.

| Letter | In the South | In the North | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| d | *y* | *z* | [[dạ]] yes (polite) |
| gi | *y* | *z* | [[gì]] what |
| v | *y*, or a soft *v* | *v* | [[vui]] happy |
| r | an *r* sound | *z* | [[rau]] vegetables |
| s | *sh*-like | *s* | [[sáu]] six |
| tr | tongue curled back | the same as *ch* | [[trà]] tea |
| qu | *w* | *kw* | [[quá]] very |

So in the South, [[dạ]], [[già]] (old) and [[vui]] all start with the same *y* sound, even though they're spelled with **d**, **gi** and **v**.

<div class="south" markdown="1">
**s** and **x** are different letters, and careful Southern speech keeps them apart: **x** like *s*, **s** more like *sh*. In relaxed speech many Southerners say both like *s*. Try [[xinh]] (pretty) and [[sinh]] (to be born).
</div>

<div class="tip" markdown="1">
For **v**, a soft English *v* is always understood, so use it if *y* feels strange. Just don't be surprised when you hear [[về]] (to go home) said like "yề".
</div>

## 4.4 Spelling rules

Three sounds change their spelling before **i, e** and **ê**:

| Sound | Before i, e, ê | Everywhere else |
|---|---|---|
| *k* | **k**: [[kem]] ice cream, [[kí]] kilogram | **c**: [[cá]] fish, [[cơm]] rice · **q** (always with *u*): [[quá]] |
| *g* | **gh**: [[ghế]] chair, [[ghi]] to write down | **g**: [[gà]] chicken, [[gỗ]] wood |
| *ng* | **ngh**: [[nghe]] to listen, [[nghỉ]] to rest | **ng**: [[ngủ]] to sleep, [[ngon]] delicious |

The sound doesn't change, only the spelling. A trick: **h** sneaks in before **i, e, ê** (*gh*, *ngh*), while *k* simply replaces *c*.

**nghe**: before *e*, the *ng* sound is spelled **ngh**. Listen: [[nghe]].
</details>

<details markdown="1">

**ghế**: before *ê*, the *g* sound is spelled **gh**. Listen: [[ghế]].
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary><em>_em</em> (ice cream): c or k?</summary>

**kem**: before *e*, the *k* sound is spelled **k**. Listen: [[kem]].
